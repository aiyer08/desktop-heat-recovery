Desktop-scale experiment

Desktop Heat Recovery

Data centers already reuse the heat from liquid cooling. This project tests the same idea on one computer: move CPU heat into a small water tank, then measure how much heat you get back and what it costs in cooling.

How it works

There are two water loops. They never mix. The heat exchanger moves heat from one to the other through thin metal plates, the same way a car's radiator moves engine heat into the air.

SVG_DIAGRAM

The radiator stays in the PC loop. Once the tank warms up it stops taking in much heat, and the CPU still needs cooling.

3D-printed parts

Each part has an editable OpenSCAD file (change the numbers at the top) and a ready-to-print STL.

Tank lid

Plugs into a 120 mm opening. Two 12.5 mm ports for inlet/outlet, two 6.5 mm probe ports (top and bottom of tank), and a vent.

Tube-probe clip ×4

Snaps onto 13 mm tubing and holds a temperature probe against it. Goes on the inlet and outlet of the heat exchanger on both loops.

Drip tray

160 × 90 mm tray with a 12 mm rim. Zip-tie slots for the heat exchanger and pump, and M4 mounting holes. Catches small leaks.

Method

  1. Baseline. Run the same one-hour CPU workload with normal cooling. Log wall power, CPU temperature, clock speed, work done per second, and room temperature.
  2. Prove the plumbing. Use a 12 V heater as the heat source first. Leak-test with the computer unplugged. The tank feeds the pump from below so the pump never runs dry.
  3. Recovery runs. Start the tank at the same temperature each time. The Raspberry Pi logs 7 probes every 5 s while the PC logs its own data.
  4. Cooldown test. Let the full tank cool for 2–3 hours. This shows how fast it leaks heat, so the result can be corrected.
  5. Repeat at least 3 times and report the average and spread.

The math

heat captured (Wh) ≈ 1.163 × litres × temperature rise (°C)

Why 1.163? Heating 1 L of water by 1 °C takes 4186 joules, and 1 watt-hour is 3600 joules. 4186 ÷ 3600 = 1.163.

23.3 Wh

Captured heat only counts as energy saved if you use it for something you would otherwise heat another way, like preheating water.
